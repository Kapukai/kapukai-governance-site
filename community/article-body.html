When power causes harm, people need a way to push back.

Tort law gives accountability another point of entry: the person harmed. Engineering can help make the evidence usable.

By Christine Hillier · Electrical Engineer & Systems Architect
Kapukai Governance Lab · October 8, 2026

Join the communityFree classes, webinars & volunteering

A person can lose their health, their livelihood, or years of their life while the institution responsible continues operating. A complaint becomes a reference number. An investigation ends. A fine is paid. The person still lives with the consequences.

One of common law’s most powerful ideas is that the injured person may have a claim of their own.

Tort law can act as a decentralized mechanism for reining in runaway power. A person with a legally recognized claim can initiate civil proceedings without waiting for a prosecutor to bring a criminal case. Accountability has another starting point.

“Decentralized” describes who can initiate the challenge. Courts still exercise public authority, and access to them is unequal. The power of the idea lies in giving the person who bears the harm a route to demand an answer.

The person harmed becomes a participant

A tort is a civil wrong for which the law provides a remedy. Negligence, intentional wrongdoing, and certain forms of strict liability are different routes with different requirements. A negligence claim generally requires a duty, a breach, causation, and injury. Strict liability does not eliminate the need to prove the elements of the particular claim. [1]

In an ordinary civil case, the burden is generally a preponderance of the evidence: more likely than not. That is different from the criminal standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt. It is not permission to replace evidence with suspicion, or proof of harm with proof of a disliked institution. [2]

For the person bringing a claim, the shift can be profound. They can seek compensation for their own injury, put their account into a formal process, and challenge the defendant’s explanation. A successful claim can connect a consequence to the party legally responsible for causing it.

The right to bring a claim is not a guarantee of a hearing, discovery, recovery, or vindication. A viable cause of action, the proper court, applicable deadlines, and procedural requirements still matter. Those access conditions belong in any honest discussion of accountability.

Power often includes control over the record

A large organization may control the measurements, internal warnings, decision logs, design specifications, and incident reports needed to understand what happened. The affected person may have only a bill, a denial, an injury, or an unexplained result.

That imbalance matters even when a regulator exists. Regulatory enforcement can impose important penalties and corrective obligations. Civil litigation can provide an additional route for an injured claimant to seek redress. Their purposes overlap, but neither automatically supplies everything the other can do.

Discovery can require the production of relevant, nonprivileged information and permit questioning under oath. In federal court, its scope is also limited by proportionality, and protective orders can restrict disclosure. [3] Discovery material does not automatically become a public archive: many discovery documents are not filed unless used in the proceeding or ordered filed. [4]

The important possibility is that an organization’s explanation can be tested against its own records. A warning, a timestamp, an ignored failure report, or a design change may turn an abstract dispute into an answerable question.

History shows the reach, and the limits

The 1998 tobacco Master Settlement Agreement resolved state litigation seeking recovery of smoking-related health care costs. It imposed continuing payment obligations and restrictions on advertising and marketing. It demonstrates how litigation can produce obligations extending beyond an isolated payment. It was a settlement with states, not a system that automatically paid every injured smoker. [5]

Opioid litigation likewise produced multiple national settlements with different defendants and terms. There is no single universal “Opioid Master Settlement” that explains every claim or every person’s recovery. The agreements and the claimant’s legal position matter. [6]

My engineering reading of these examples is straightforward: accountability is stronger when the consequences reach the decisions that produced the harm. A large payment alone does not demonstrate that a hazardous process has been repaired. Correction needs its own evidence.

Engineering can reduce the distance between harm and proof

Software and data tools can help people organize material that would otherwise be expensive or difficult to examine. EPA’s ECHO services, for example, provide programmatic access to environmental compliance and enforcement data. [7] CourtListener provides legal data services and documentation; access terms and coverage must be checked for the intended use. Neither service represents a complete record of every relevant event. [8]

The practical contribution is to make a claim inspectable. An engineer can preserve original records, align dates, trace transformations, compare locations, identify missing measurements, and test competing explanations. Automation can reduce repetitive work while keeping each result connected to its source.

Consider a hypothetical water contamination investigation. A map showing illness near a facility may justify further inquiry. Establishing causation may also require evidence about the substance, release dates, transport pathways, exposure, dose, health outcomes, and alternative causes. A compelling visualization cannot fill those gaps by itself.

Code can make an argument reproducible. It cannot make an unsupported argument true.

Federal Rule of Evidence 702 requires expert testimony to rest on sufficient facts or data, reliable methods, and reliable application to the case. An engineer’s model must explain its assumptions and limits. The rule does not give software a shortcut around evidentiary scrutiny. [9]

The same discipline applies to AI. A model can extract a date incorrectly, merge two people, omit a denial, or turn an allegation into a finding. Useful automation must preserve attribution, expose uncertainty, and support correction. The number of pages processed is not a measure of truth.

Access to evidence is part of access to remedy

The promise of private enforcement weakens when only wealthy parties can assemble an intelligible record. Filing costs, technical complexity, delay, and unequal access to expertise can make a formal right difficult to exercise.

Tort law also does not provide an unrestricted route against every exercise of government power. Sovereign immunity and its waivers can determine whether a claim against a government may proceed. A technical demonstration of error does not, by itself, establish an available legal remedy. [10]

For Kapukai, the design question is practical: how can more people preserve what happened, distinguish what they know from what they infer, and prepare information that another person can meaningfully review?

That work can begin before anyone decides whether litigation is appropriate. A usable chronology, a faithful statement, a source index, and a documented correction request can make the next conversation more precise.

Build the capacity to question power

Tort law’s contribution is the possibility that a person who suffers a legally recognized injury can initiate a demand for accountability. Engineering’s contribution is the ability to make complex evidence easier to examine, reproduce, and correct.

Bringing those capabilities together requires care from both disciplines. Lawyers assess claims and procedure. Qualified experts evaluate technical questions. Affected people retain their voice. Independent reviewers describe what they actually observed and disclose what they could not verify.

A system is more accountable when the people affected by it can inspect the record, question the decision, and pursue a usable path to correction.
